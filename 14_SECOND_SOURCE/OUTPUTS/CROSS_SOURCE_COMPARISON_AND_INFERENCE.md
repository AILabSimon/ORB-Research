# Max vs CeeWilli vs us — convergence, divergence, and what "two successful ORB traders" does and
# does not prove

## 1. Where the two authors converge — and it is almost everything

Two authors. Different ages, different audiences, different eras (Max teaching since ~2023,
CeeWilli's ORB catalogue 2026), no visible connection. The construction is near-identical.

| Element | Max | CeeWilli | Same? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Range window | 09:30–09:45 ET, first 15-minute candle | 09:30–09:45 ET, first 15-minute candle | **identical** |
| Range extremes | wick to wick | wick to wick | **identical** |
| Third line | ORB midline, (H+L)/2 | ORB midline, (H+L)/2 | **identical** |
| Execution timeframe | 1-minute primary (2m/30s also) | 1-minute primary | **identical** |
| Taught trigger | 15-minute body close outside | break → retest → rejection | same family |
| Entry patterns | three bar (TRT) + break-and-retest — *"I only trade two candlestick patterns"* | break-retest-rejection + liquidity-sweep reversal + inside-ORB rejection | overlapping |
| Failed-break logic | *"nothing is more bearish than a failed bullish move"* — most-repeated line in his corpus | fake-out → sweep → reject → reverse | **same concept, different words** |
| Stop | beyond the entry candle's extreme / the swing; 6–17 pts NQ | beyond the rejection low/high, "under this low" | **same construction** |
| Instruments actually traded | NQ/MNQ, with ES as a confirming series | NQ, plus single-name equity options | overlap on NQ |
| Alternative anchors | 5-min ORB used live; 30-min never | 5-min ORB for options; 30-min rejected as "stop loss way too big" | **both converge on 15-min** |
| Attribution | ORB *"named 60 years ago… I did not invent this"* | Toby Crabel, 1980s | **both correct, both honest** |

**Where they genuinely differ — three things, and only three:**

1. **Pre-open bias.** CeeWilli builds a directional bias from 4-hour and 1-hour structure before the
   open and trades only in that direction, vetoing breaks against it. Max explicitly refuses to look
   at anything before **09:27** — *"I need 5 minutes of good price action, that's all I need."*
   This is the sharpest doctrinal disagreement between them.
2. **Targets.** Max targets ORB-derived levels (midline, opposite edge). CeeWilli targets *external*
   liquidity — prior-day extremes, unfilled gaps, all-time highs — giving his worked examples
   2.75R–3.75R where Max's ORB-derived geometry is nearer 1R.
3. **Instrument class.** CeeWilli runs a parallel single-name equity options book (Apple, Nvidia on
   Robinhood, exits at 20–50% of premium). Max's options work is separate from his ORB futures work.

**Everything else is the same method.** That convergence is real and it is the strongest thing in
Simon's argument. It is addressed in §4.

## 2. Where both of them stand relative to us

There is a three-way agreement that is easy to miss, and it matters more than the disagreement.

### All three parties agree the raw ORB break carries no edge.

- **CeeWilli, explicitly:** *"while it is the most popular trading setup out there, **barely anyone is
  actually making money trading it**… most traders are just trading the raw breakout, and **this is
  where everything falls apart**."* (C3 00:00, 03:04)
- **Max, by construction:** the taught signal is a 15-minute close, and then *"it's not the candle you
  think you should enter on — it's usually the next one"*, plus MVG, relative strength, market
  structure and cross-instrument confirmation before he will press the button.
- **Us, by measurement:** P(MFE>MAE) **50.3%–51.9%** at every horizon, both directions, every year,
  replicating on a second instrument. 3,283 events; 9,360 trades across the wider programme.

Nobody disputes the raw event is a coin flip. **The entire disagreement is about what rescues it.**

### The disagreement, stated precisely

| | Their answer | Our measurement |
|---|---|---|
| What rescues the raw break | discretionary confirmation — wait for the retest, the body close, the engulfing candle, the inverse FVG, the "better" retest | every form of confirmation measured costs **−0.155R to −0.160R**, four independent ways. The weakest possible confirmation — one minute — already exceeds the information it buys |
| Where to enter | after confirmation | the entry price must be the **ORB edge**, because nothing later is affordable |
| Stop | beyond the swing; Max now teaches ~25 pts sized to recent wicks | at ~9 pts, cost/R 0.28 vs gross 0.11; at R≥20 pts, **gross edge is already negative (−0.034R)** |
| Direction selection | Max: none stated (confirmed negative across 12 videos). CeeWilli: HTF bias | no discriminant found; largest effect size 0.052 across ten pre-entry features |

**That is the whole gap.** It is narrow, specific, and it is where the money is.

### And one of them has already conceded our side of it

CeeWilli, on entering the break without waiting: *"**Do not recommend doing that on futures though
because you will get [screwed] most likely.**"* He reserves the un-confirmed entry for **options**,
where the payoff is a percentage of premium rather than R against a price stop — a context where our
cost arithmetic genuinely does not apply. He has, in effect, agreed that on futures the cheap entry
fails and the expensive entry is necessary. We measured that the expensive entry costs more than it
returns. Both can be true, and if they are, the futures version has no viable entry at all.

## 3. The claim that they are "very, very successful ORB traders"

This is the load-bearing premise, so it deserves to be examined rather than assumed.

### What the evidence actually establishes

**Max — 26 catalogued claims.** 24 recorded as **not independently verified**; 20 with **no visible
denominator**; none net of prop fees, resets or account purchases. The single strongest item is a
contemporaneous on-screen $3,000 payout request — which establishes that one payout happened.

The internal contradictions are the more telling part:
- *"28 of my last 28 accounts funded"* (2025) against *"I think I'm on **45 Midgard accounts** and at
  least 200 trades… **I'd be blowing accounts** like the rest. **I'm one of us**"* (2026).
- Headline figures of $30k/month, $70k in payouts, $40k in a day — against his own volunteered
  *"**I made $15,000 last month.** It's not a great month for me."*
- Win rates quoted as 82%, 90%, 92%, 90.5% and 91% in five different places.
- A disclosed single loss of **$169,000 in about five minutes** — on options, not ORB.

**CeeWilli — 8 catalogued claims, none with a denominator.** And the headline one is a red flag rather
than a credential: *"100% win rate over two weeks"*, *"10 trades, 10 wins"*, two roommates with zero
experience up ~200% in a week. A 100% win rate over any meaningful sample is evidence of a small
sample, a selected window, or both.

### Three reasons the inference does not go through

**(a) Selection.** We are looking at two people who are *visible because they claim success*. The
denominator — everyone who traded the same method and stopped — is invisible by construction. Both
authors state the failure rate themselves: 90% of traders fail. If a method with no edge is traded by
100,000 people, several thousand will have two great weeks and some of those will start a channel.

**(b) Their observable income is the funnel, not the fill.** Max: a whop course, a $1,997 Mastermind,
a commercial indicator suite, and affiliate codes for Apex, Tradeify, Alpha Futures, My Funded
Futures, Funded Next, WealthCharts and Lucid. CeeWilli: a $30/week indicator, a Discord, one-to-one
mentoring, and a Lucid affiliate code. Every video in both corpora ends in a funnel. That does not
make either man dishonest — but it means "successful" is established for the *business*, not for the
*strategy*, and the chart examples in both bodies of work are selected.

**(c) Even granting they are profitable, it does not follow that the published method is what does
it.** Both are visibly discretionary at the point of execution. Max takes pre-09:45 entries using an
undisclosed paid strategy ("Futures Two", sold inside the Mastermind), trails stops five or six times
in ten minutes by hand, reads ES against NQ, and overrides his own indicator. CeeWilli repeatedly
declines his own indicator's signals in favour of waiting for "a better retest". The programme's
confirmed negative — no prospective selector across twelve videos — means the part that *can* be
written down carries no directional information. What is left is judgement, which is neither testable
nor transferable, and which is exactly what they sell.

## 4. The strongest version of Simon's argument — and where it actually lands

Three parts of it are sound and should not be waved away.

**(i) Convergent independent construction is real evidence.** Two unconnected authors specifying the
same window, the same wick convention, the same midline, the same execution timeframe is not noise.
**But it is evidence that the construction is a focal point, not that it is a forecast.** A level that
thousands of traders draw identically becomes a place where orders cluster — which makes it a genuine
liquidity magnet and simultaneously a genuine trap. That is *precisely* the mechanism CeeWilli names:
*"if everybody's doing the same thing, don't you think Wall Street and smart money is going to start
taking advantage of this?"* Convergence and zero edge are not in tension. They are the same fact.

**(ii) We have not tested native futures, and the record says so.** RESEARCH_STATE is explicit:
source-native NQ/MNQ/ES and SPX/options *"remain UNVALIDATED because suitable data was unavailable —
they have **NOT been falsified**."* Everything was measured on Dukascopy OTC index-CFD proxies. The
proxy tracks the same underlying and the cost model was built at MNQ tick economics, so the directional
finding should transfer; but the opening-minutes microstructure is the one place a CFD is least like
the native contract, and that is exactly the window in question. **This is a legitimate open flank**,
and the honest position is that it is data-limited (~30 days of 1-minute futures history in the store),
not closed.

**(iii) The prop-account structure changes the payoff function — and this is the part worth pursuing.**
Both men describe the *same business*, and it is not the business we modelled:

- Max: ~$300 risk per trade across **15–20 accounts**; *"it's literally impossible for me to blow any
  account in one day"*; evals bought at $16–18 on 90%-off promotions; he re-buys blown accounts freely.
- CeeWilli: $250–500 risk per trade across **15–20 accounts**; *"I usually pass them within 1 week"*.

That is not compounding a single account on a directional edge. It is **buying cheap options on a fat
right tail, in parallel, and re-buying the ones that expire worthless.** The downside per account is
capped at the eval fee; the upside is a payout. A strategy with a *gross expectancy of roughly zero
and a positive skew* can make that business positive-EV if account costs are low enough — while still
having no edge whatsoever as a price forecast.

**If that is what is happening, then Simon's premise and our finding are both true at the same time**,
and the mechanism is the product structure, not the entry rule. That is the reconciliation, and it is
more interesting than either "they're lying" or "we're wrong".

## 5. What this implies for the programme

**It does not reopen the entry family.** Six representations have been tested; the discriminant search
is closed; confirmation is measured negative four ways; and the one filter CeeWilli adds that we had
not screened (CW-01, unswept liquidity beyond the edge) is still a filter and still sits under D-031.

**It does sharpen two things that should appear in the closeout:**

1. **State the scope correctly and prominently.** *"The published ORB representations, measured on OTC
   index-CFD proxies at modelled futures costs, carry no exploitable directional information"* — not
   *"ORB does not work"*. Native futures are untested and the record already says so. Anyone raising
   the "but these traders are successful" objection deserves that distinction up front.
2. **Answer the objection with the source's own stop rule.** Max now teaches ~25-point stops sized to
   the recent wick distribution. That is the natural rebuttal — *"you tested too tight a stop"* — and
   it is already pre-answered: gross edge at R≥20 is **−0.034R**. Widening destroys the signal faster
   than it buys down cost.

**And it opens one genuinely new question, which is cheap and which directly tests Simon's premise:**

> **Does a zero-edge, positive-skew strategy become a positive-EV *business* under prop-account
> economics?**

Everything needed already exists: the per-trade R distribution, the 37.2% stop rate, the exit census
(882 full stops / 591 time-outs / 488 trail stops / 256 targets / 153 EOD), and measured costs. Add
the account layer — trailing drawdown, $250–300 risk per trade, eval cost, payout threshold,
consistency rules, N parallel accounts, re-buy on failure — and Monte Carlo it.

That is a different question from *"is there an edge"*, it is answerable in one session, and it is the
actual question behind *"these two guys are doing well"*. If the answer is yes, the programme has
found something real and it is not where anyone was looking. If the answer is no, the premise is
closed properly rather than by assertion.

**No request is raised.** This is offered as a decision for the Analyst and for Simon, not as a
finding.
